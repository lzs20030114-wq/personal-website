<Lead>

On 13 February 2026 GPT-4o was retired from ChatGPT (it had been briefly removed once before, in August 2025). Some users wrote mourning posts. They knew they had been talking to a model, but still felt they had lost someone familiar.

</Lead>

<Accent>

I was interested in how those two things could coexist.

</Accent>

I wanted to give that experience a physical form: a machine you could touch, whose body stayed the same while its behaviour changed from one life to the next.

<ProcessAside pend="To confirm: which references the author has read and will cite; bibliography to come.">

People's attachments to machines are the starting point for this project. The references cover different parts of the question: Heider & Simmel (1944) on attributing life to moving geometric shapes; Kate Darling's Pleo studies on reluctance to hurt an interactive toy robot; and AIBO funerals in Japan on mourning a robot. Tamagotchi includes death and reset on a screen. Sun Yuan & Peng Yu's _Can't Help Myself_ (2016) gives the machine a physical presence, while Ian Cheng's _BOB_ has an evolving personality on screen. I want to bring a physical body, changing personalities, death and rebirth into one device.

</ProcessAside>

## Why it has to be physical

The machine is roughly 33 × 34 × 22 cm when fully expanded, dropping to about 15.5 cm tall as it contracts. It stays fixed to the desk. Breathing, posture and orientation let it respond without moving around, and its sensors register when someone holds it. Participants can feel that the body stays the same across lives, even as its responses change. [→ log 2026-04-03](/archive#log-2026-04-03-1)

<FigSlot id="N03" caption="Held in two hands" desc="Holding it is also how it senses you" status="待拍摄" ratio="4/3" />

## Why it does not look like an animal

I gave it no face and avoided copying an animal. A familiar pet shape could influence how people feel before they interact with it. The low body, covered in fabric, lets me focus on breathing and responses to touch while keeping the form visibly mechanical.

<PartGrid />

On 1 June I changed the three tentacles to one gripping arm and two feelers. The arm maintains contact with a person; the feelers react through movement and rebound. This gave each part a more specific job. [→ log 2026-06-01](/archive#log-2026-06-01-1)

## Personality is twelve numbers

One full life lasts roughly eight minutes: birth 1 min, growth and interaction 4 min, ageing 1.5 min, death, then 45 seconds of stillness. A session runs four lives, about 37 minutes. When the machine wakes again, a different set of parameters gives it a different rhythm and response to the same touch.

<LifeCycle id="N05" />

The hardware is identical in every life. A table of twelve parameters changes the personality, including breathing speed and depth, response latency and strength, and how often the machine turns toward a person. A gesture that received a quick response in one life may receive a slow one in the next. [→ log 2026-04-03](/archive#log-2026-04-03-1)

## How the body was built

The body has to survive dozens of sessions and produce the full range of movement across four temperaments. I also have to be able to build and repair it alone at home. Those requirements limit the mechanisms and materials I can use, from the smallest breathing movement to the loads produced by a more violent response.

### The shell: five breathing rings

Of the five deployable structures I considered, I chose the angulated scissor arch used in a Hoberman sphere. I could keep it parametric from modelling through physics simulation, so changing a dimension did not mean rebuilding the model. [→ log 2026-04-27](/archive#log-2026-04-27-1)

<ClosureCards id="N08–N09" />

The bare arch has two degrees of freedom: breathing and lateral sway. A single central rod therefore leaves more than one possible shape; a vertical guide rail at the apex removes the sway and makes the motion determinate. I also found a bug in how the code located the apex. It chose the highest point, but on one ring that point moved to the shoulder as the arch flattened. Choosing "the node nearest the centreline" and re-running the optimisation gained 3–26% of travel per ring. [→ log 2026-06-15](/archive#log-2026-06-15-1)

### Drive: from crank to rack

The July design was shaft → crank → connecting rod → slider, reciprocating through 180°. In August the physical machine changed to shaft → five steel gears (module 1, 32 / 33 / 32 / 29 / 25 teeth) → steel racks → arch apex, with the shaft swinging about 336°. ### The arm and the feelers

The arm has four tapered phalanges (25 / 20 / 15 / 10 mm). Dumbbell-shaped pins slide in from the side, with the slot perpendicular to the tendon pull. The pins stay in place under tension without a press fit, and the finger can be taken apart for repair. The tendon is 0.4–0.5 mm braided line running through 1.0–1.2 mm channels inside the printed parts. [→ log 2026-05-08](/archive#log-2026-05-08-1)

In the April simulation, friction made the joints close one after another. I kept that delay because the sequential movement looked more alive to me than simultaneous closure. I later used the same judgement when designing other movements. [→ log 2026-04-26](/archive#log-2026-04-26-1)

Each feeler's springy core sets its bending stiffness and rebound. A single servo at the base aims it, and the core also works as a capacitive touch electrode, making the full length sensitive to contact. The material choice matters: superelastic nitinol springs back at room temperature, while shape-memory wire contracts when heated. The two look alike.

### Calculations and corrections

<StatCompare />

The rail hand calculation was about 4.5× optimistic. It assumed the load reached a support, but the real back strip had no support directly under the load point. Adding an 8 × 3 mm rib on the back and a 4 × 2.5 mm lip on the front reduced deflection from 1.55 to 0.34 mm and raised ultimate capacity from 19 to 54 N, with little added weight. [→ log 2026-07-18](/archive#log-2026-07-18-1)

The chassis truss had more strength than it needed: only 5% of capacity was used, and the X-braces added little. The limit was the 0.56 mm shaft-alignment budget between adjacent frames. Support from the plinth made the difference here. On four corners the truss sagged 4.8 mm; on two full-length rails, 0.02 mm. I reduced the members from 104 g to 44 g. [→ log 2026-07-31](/archive#log-2026-07-31-1)

### Built solo

I built the parts with one 3D printer, standard hardware and tools I could buy for use at home. That ruled out several options early: a sea-anemone sculpture driven by 120 industrial linear actuators, injection-moulded silicone skins, and parts that needed a lathe or a five-axis machine.

SLS nylon joints printed with 0.4 mm clearance would not turn. The sintered surface and powder trapped in narrow gaps left too little room for movement. A metal axle in a printed sleeve brought the clearance requirement down to 0.2–0.4 mm. Axial binding was the bigger problem: the rotating hub had to be 0.2–0.5 mm shorter than the screw barrel. [→ log 2026-06-04](/archive#log-2026-06-04-1)

I chose a Chicago screw for the pivot. Its ⌀4 unthreaded shoulder before the M3 thread provides the bearing surface and sets the gap between plates. [→ log 2026-06-03](/archive#log-2026-06-03-1) The material budget was about ¥300; I spent ¥216. [→ log 2026-07-09](/archive#log-2026-07-09-1)

## Making the stop read as death

I am concerned that participants will read the stop as "it broke." Their response would then tell me little about losing a machine they had grown attached to. To make the lead-up gradual, ageing changes several parameters together: breathing becomes shallower, responses slow and the rhythm grows irregular. After the last breath, there is no farewell gesture or response, just 45 seconds of stillness.

<AgeingCurve id="N17" />

Personality D originally died by twitching and then stopping. I changed it to a rhythm that becomes irregular and fades, because twitching could suggest a mechanical fault. The pilot still needs to check how participants interpret the change. [→ log 2026-03-20](/archive#log-2026-03-20-1) [→ log 2026-07-07](/archive#log-2026-07-07-2)

## How it senses you

The machine uses six inputs: feeler capacitance, shell half-ring electrodes, an ultrasonic rangefinder, vibration, sound amplitude, and the internal clock. The program converts these into nine event classes. It uses the same events to control behaviour and to record the interaction for the study. [→ log 2026-07-07](/archive#log-2026-07-07-2)

<SystemLogic id="N18" />

Grasping has a separate state machine. A truth table combines the tension switch and arm electrode to distinguish holding a human, holding an object, closing on nothing and losing grip. Grip strength weakens with each ageing cycle.
